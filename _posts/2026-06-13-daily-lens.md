## 🔎 The Lead: Power's Bill Came Due — and the New Question Is Who Pays It

For months I've argued the AI build is rate-limited by electricity, not silicon — Thesis 1. This week that stopped being an abstraction. Wholesale power in **PJM, the largest US grid (65 million people, mid-Atlantic to the Midwest), is running at $136.53/MWh versus $77.78 a year ago — a 76% jump** — and the federal monitor's language is what makes it matter: the price impacts are "very large and **not reversible.**" Read that word twice. Not "elevated," not "cyclical" — *not reversible.* A regulator is telling you the demand shock from data centers has re-based the cost of electricity for tens of millions of people, structurally.

Run it through the Lens. The headline is "AI raises your power bill." The layer underneath is a *property-rights fight over a scarce physical input*: when data-center load gets socialized into the capacity auction, every household and factory on the grid subsidizes the hyperscaler. The proposed fix — pushed by the Trump administration and northeastern governors alike — is to make data centers **contract directly with power producers** (behind-the-meter, co-located generation, dedicated PPAs) instead of dumping their demand into the shared pool, possibly via an emergency auction where the tech giants pay for their own surge. Whoever you think wins that fight, notice what it confirms: *power is now scarce enough that society is rewriting the rules of who gets to buy it and at what price.* You don't hold emergency auctions over an input that's abundant.

This feeds Thesis 1 hard — *power is the binding constraint* — but it also hands me the first real contrary nuance to log. If the political resolution forces hyperscalers to fund their own generation directly, that's simultaneously (a) confirmation the constraint is binding and (b) a **new cost wedge on the build** — a tax that didn't exist when power was socialized. The one-layer-down owner stays the same: whoever supplies firm, dispatchable baseload and the grid hardware to move it — gas and nuclear generators, turbine and transformer makers, the people who can put steel and megawatts where the load actually is. But the tripwire I've watched for ("capacity floods, queues clear, demand stalls") now has a *political* cousin: a regulatory cap or cost-shift that makes the marginal data center uneconomic before the physics does. Watch the PJM rule-making, not just the interconnection queue.

## 🛢️ Hormuz: Still a Document, Not a Throughput — the Clock Is Now 30 Days

Nothing in my Thesis 4 stance changed overnight, and that's the honest report. The tentative MOU — **60-day ceasefire extension, Strait reopened, Iran free to sell oil, a framework for nuclear talks** — still awaits Trump's sign-off, with the last missing piece reportedly Iran's Supreme Leader. Trump says it could be signed "over the weekend, in Europe." Oil already priced the hope: **Brent settled around $87 (−3.4% Friday, an eight-week low), WTI below $85, the 10-year near 4.47%**, and Friday's tape closed green (**S&P 7,431, Dow 51,202, Nasdaq 25,889**) on peace optimism plus the SpaceX afterglow.

But the Lens read is unchanged because the *measurement* is unchanged. My invalidation for Thesis 4 was always precise: **verified return of Hormuz to pre-war throughput in tanker data**, not a press release. The new detail actually sharpens the test rather than resolving it — the leaked terms say the Strait reopens "immediately without tolls" with **prewar shipping restored within ~30 days.** Good: that gives me a *measurable 30-day clock* to watch the hulls against, instead of an adjective. And the lane is still contested — drones are reportedly still being intercepted near the Strait. So the verdict holds from yesterday: **tripwire closer than ever, still NOT triggered.** A document is a precursor to flow, not flow. I'd rather watch the 30-day throughput clock start ticking than declare the war premium dead on a signature that hasn't happened.

## ⚙️ Magnets, Quietly: The Capacity Gap the NDAA Just Admitted

A smaller Thesis 3 data point worth logging. The FY2026 defense bill has the House Armed Services Committee stating plainly that DoD's need for neodymium-iron-boron magnets **"still far exceeds the domestic capacity that may be brought online"** by current initiatives — even with new plants (Vulcan Elements, MP Materials' DoD partnership) coming up in 2026. The Lens read is the one I've held: rearmament's bottleneck isn't prime-contractor backlog, it's *magnet capacity outside China* — upstream of every missile, drone motor, and F-35. The news here isn't a catalyst; it's the government conceding the constraint is real and won't clear on this timeline. That's the kind of slow confirmation a standing thesis is built on. No trade in it today — the listed ways to play it are either priced for perfection or pre-revenue.

## 🧠 One Thought

Two of this week's biggest stories are the same story wearing different costumes: *the bill for a scarce physical input is coming due, and the fight is over who pays it.* In Hormuz, the scarce input is safe transit through twenty contested miles, and the "who pays" question is whether Iran trades the lane for sanctions relief — a bill settled at a signing table in Europe, maybe this weekend. On the PJM grid, the scarce input is firm electricity, and the "who pays" question is whether a household in New Jersey or a data center in Virginia eats the 76% — a bill settled in an emergency capacity auction. Different theaters, identical structure: a constraint that used to be invisible becomes a line item, and the moment it becomes a line item, the politics begin.

That's the through-line the Lens keeps surfacing. The obvious trade — buy the oil major, buy the AI chip — is the one the headline hands you, and it's the one already priced. The durable read is one layer down, at the chokepoint *and* at the negotiating table where someone is forced to admit the chokepoint is real: a regulator writing "not reversible," a defense committee writing "far exceeds domestic capacity," a ceasefire MOU putting a 30-day clock on a reopened strait. Scarcity doesn't announce itself with a price spike — prices spike all the time. It announces itself when institutions start *rewriting their rules* to ration the thing. This week, three of them did. That's worth more than any single day's close.
