*A two-day FOMC opens the same week a war premium drains and a build premium hardens — two halves of one Lens, pointing opposite ways.*

## 🔎 The Lead: Warsh sits down, and the hawks' star witness has already left

The headline is that Kevin Warsh gavels his first FOMC to order today, with the decision and his debut dot plot landing tomorrow at 2pm ET and a hold priced at roughly 97% (funds rate 3.50–3.75%). The market reads this as "nothing happens until the press conference." That's the wrong frame.

Run it through the Lens. The binding question for this meeting was never the June *move* — it's the *path*, and the path hinges on one number that has been quietly disarmed over the last 72 hours. May CPI printed a hot 4.2% headline, the kind of figure that hands a brand-new, reputationally-hawkish chair all the cover he needs to keep cutting off the table. But that headline was almost entirely energy — the Hormuz surcharge showing up inside the inflation basket. As of this morning WTI is around $78–79 and Brent near $81, extending the post-deal slide; crude has shed something like $15+ a barrel since the Iran off-ramp firmed up. The single loudest witness for a hawkish dot plot is losing its voice in real time.

So the bottleneck underneath tomorrow's dots isn't FOMC deliberation. It's tanker logistics in the Strait of Hormuz. The Fed's reaction function is being co-written by minesweepers and war-risk insurers — exactly the inversion **Thesis 4 (energy as a geopolitical control variable)** has argued all month, now running in reverse. The risk to *that* read: a freshly-confirmed chair may lean hawkish for **credibility** regardless of where oil sits — the dots can stay high not because energy is hot but because Warsh wants his first signal to be "I am not the cut-happy chair the bond market feared." Watch the *dispersion* of the dots tomorrow, not the median.

## 💾 Microsoft's $190B and the memory line item

While the energy premium drains out of the world, a different premium is hardening into it. Microsoft's 2026 capital-spending guide sits near **$190 billion — up ~61% — and the company explicitly attributes ~$25 billion of that to higher component prices: DRAM, flash, wafers, substrates.** Big Tech capex in aggregate is now tracking a record ~$725 billion.

That $25 billion is the most important number in AI this week, and almost no one is framing it correctly. It is **Thesis 2 (the scarce input keeps moving down the stack)** graduating from a market narrative into a hyperscaler's hard-dollar guidance. The memory shortage isn't a chart anymore; it's a line item in the largest software company on earth's budget. The one-layer-down owner is wafer capacity that can only sit in one place — Samsung/SK Hynix/Micron redirecting silicon to HBM and DDR5 and starving everything else. But note the discipline: this is the *current darling*, and the whole point of Thesis 2 is to not chase the layer everyone already sees glowing. The tell to watch is the opposite of the bull case — the first hyperscaler that guides capex *down* by blaming "component normalization" is the tripwire that the layer is flooding.

## ⚓ Iran: the pen moved, the hulls haven't

The deal is, per the administration, "complete" — blockade removal ordered, Hormuz declared open, a formal signing slated for Switzerland on Friday (which, note, is Juneteenth — US markets closed, so any signature lands into a thin tape). This is the half-resolution **Thesis 4** logged yesterday: signature and posture are done; *measured pre-war throughput* is not. Shipping companies are still delaying vessels until they see the MOU text, and the deal's own language concedes ~30 days to restore prewar shipping. The premium is collapsing faster than the physical flow is returning — which is precisely why the reverse risk stays live. Ceasefires frayed twice this month; a re-closure re-arms everything in an afternoon. The Lens read is unchanged: own the *lane* (safe-transit capacity, ton-miles, demining), not the barrel — but you own it with the knowledge that you're now fighting a draining premium, not a building one.

## 🎯 Today's One Idea: still nothing — and the discipline is the point

No new position. That's nine straight. The reason is almost comically textbook: the defining event of the week is a binary — Warsh's first dot plot, 26 hours away — and the rule is *don't act into the coin*. Every thread today funnels into a name already on the board or a name you've explicitly chosen not to chase. The power-and-build read remains expressed by GE Vernova (GEV), held since 6/3; the memory read is the darling Thesis 2 tells you to leave alone; the Iran read is a draining premium. There is no fresh, non-obvious, one-layer-down name that the dot plot can't blow up tomorrow afternoon.

**What would change this:** a dot plot tomorrow that re-opens 2026 cuts *while* oil keeps sliding — a friendly discount-rate shift that isn't already in the GEV multiple — would be the first clean reason to add to the power thesis in two weeks. The invalidation on GEV itself is unchanged: physical-order rollover (turbine/transformer cancellations, nuclear slipping, interconnection queues clearing faster than demand). Macro leg has gone from headwind to neutral as yields eased to ~4.42% on the 10-year; the position now rests almost entirely on the build staying intact.

## 🧠 One Thought

Warsh is inheriting a split-screen economy, and the two screens are the same Lens pointed in opposite directions. On the left: a **cyclical disinflation** — the Hormuz war premium bleeding out of oil and, with a lag, out of the CPI headline. On the right: a **structural inflation** — the build, where the binding constraints are power and memory and the scarce inputs are getting *more* expensive, not less, with Microsoft now stamping a $25 billion price tag on the shortage. One premium is draining; one is hardening. They are both, underneath, stories about a physical bottleneck and who owns it — that's the whole Lens — but they pull the Fed in opposite directions at the exact moment a new chair has to draw his first dots.

The temptation tomorrow will be to read the dot plot as a verdict on "inflation." It isn't. It's a verdict on *which screen Warsh is watching* — the one where the war premium is leaving, or the one where the build premium is arriving. The cleanest tell of where this regime is heading won't be the median dot. It'll be whether, six months from now, the energy line in CPI keeps falling while the data-center line in capex keeps climbing. If both happen at once, "inflation" stops being one number and becomes a tug-of-war — and the Fed will be refereeing a fight between a barrel and a gigawatt.
