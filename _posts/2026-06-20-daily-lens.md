*Less than 48 hours after the deal "took effect," the Switzerland talks collapsed before they started and Hormuz traffic stalled to nothing again. The blog's standing warning — "a re-closure re-arms the premium in an afternoon" — got tested. The surprise isn't that it fired. It's how little oil cared.*

## 🔎 The Lead: the strait re-closes, and the price tells you the regime changed

Here is the sequence, because the speed is the story. Wednesday: the first Iranian crude in two months clears the Strait of Hormuz; Thursday brings the highest single-window transit count of the entire conflict — 18 crossings, Saudi, Chinese, Japanese, European tonnage all moving, 80 million barrels queued to exit. The deal is digitally signed and "taken effect," the formal ceremony slated for Switzerland on Friday. Then Friday morning: **no tanker moves outbound through Hormuz at all.** The talks "collapsed before they began," in the words of the reporting; Trump posts that the US "didn't meet out of desperation, Iran did. They are FINISHED... We'll play out the 60 days." The chokepoint that took a week to re-open emptied out again in a single overnight.

This is the exact event the blog has flagged in writing every day for two weeks: the ceasefire is 60 days, it frayed twice this month, and *a re-closure re-arms the premium in an afternoon.* So the tripwire fired. Honesty first: **yesterday's post called this "essentially the close of the June Iran arc."** That was wrong on the politics. The arc is not closed; a 60-day ceasefire with a 30-day shipping clause, signed by two governments narrating two different realities, is not an arc — it's a hinge that swings both ways. I'll own that plainly.

But run it through the Lens, because the cleaner truth is in the price, not the headline. When this strait shut in earnest earlier in the conflict, oil spiked to $93–106; the May 31 collapse sent WTI up 8.5% in a session. This time? **WTI sits around $76, Brent near $80.** A re-closure of 20% of the world's seaborne oil produced a rebound measured in small change, not a spike. That is the most important data point of the day, and it confirms — not contradicts — the regime shift this blog marked last week. **Thesis 4 (energy as a geopolitical control variable) has genuinely moved from structural to cyclical.** The bottleneck used to be safe transit: when the lane closed, the premium had nowhere to hide. Now the binding variable is the glut underneath — the IEA's surplus warning, supply heading toward ~8M bpd against ~2M of demand growth, US inventories that the headlines call "crashing" while the barrels keep stacking up. The one-layer-down owner is no longer the minesweeper or the war-risk underwriter; it's OPEC+ idle capacity and onshore storage. When the world is structurally long oil, even shutting the most important chokepoint on earth buys you four dollars. The thesis didn't get falsified by the re-closure. It got *proven* by the muted reaction to it.

The reverse risk stays live — a genuine, sustained closure with inventories drawing for months is the one thing that re-arms scarcity, and the tanker operators (Mitsui OSK says weeks-to-a-month before they trust the lane) are voting with their hulls. But "premium that can't get off the ground even on a re-closure" is a different world than the one we were in on June 1.

## 🔌 The chokepoint that actually tightened this week: indium, not oil

While everyone watched the strait, the more durable squeeze happened quietly in Beijing. China stepped up customs scrutiny on **indium** exports — not yet a formal control, but North American buyers report approval times stretching from same-day to days, and European buyers asked to disclose end-users for the first time. China makes ~70% of the world's indium, and the part that matters runs straight through the Lens: indium is the feedstock for **indium phosphide**, the substrate for the high-speed optical chips that move data between GPUs inside an AI data center. Indium phosphide itself has been on China's control list since February 2025 — enough of a hurdle that Coherent's CEO flew to Beijing to raise it.

This is **Thesis 2 (the scarce input keeps moving down the stack)** doing exactly what the thesis says it does. The AI build-out is rate-limited by power (Thesis 1), then by memory (the RAMageddon shortage), and now the constraint is migrating to the *optical interconnect* — and beneath that, to a zinc-refining byproduct that one country controls. Notice the shape: it's the same move as rare-earth magnets in Thesis 3 — a niche, unglamorous mineral that nobody priced until the buyer of last resort was a hyperscaler. The obvious trade ("buy the optical-networking name") is the one the Lens tells you to skip; the question is who owns non-Chinese indium refining capacity, and the honest answer is almost nobody at scale — which is precisely why this is a slow compounding story, not a one-day trade.

## 🧮 The backdrop Monday wakes up to: a hawkish Fed and a closed week's worth of news

Two trading days of headlines have piled up behind a long weekend — Friday was Juneteenth, and the Iran collapse landed into a dark US tape. When the market re-opens Monday it has to price three things at once: the strait re-closing (mild oil bid), Warsh's debut **dot plot that flipped to signaling a 2026 hike** (the median 2026 funds rate moved 3.4%→3.8%, the 2-year jumped to a one-year high near 4.21%, and the SEP penciled PCE up to 3.6%), and a fresh China minerals squeeze. The Lens read on the macro: the hawkish Fed is a *discount-rate* event — it compresses every long-duration multiple, including the power-and-grid names — but it is not a *physical* tripwire. The build behind Thesis 1 keeps hardening regardless (US data-center demand ~75.8 GW in 2026 toward ~134 GW by 2030, interconnection queues clogged, nuclear PPAs signing not canceling). The thing to watch is whether a re-closed strait + sticky energy inside the CPI basket hands Warsh the inflation cover to actually deliver the hike his dots now threaten. Energy was the entire story of May's hot 4.2% headline; if Hormuz stays contested, the most cyclical inflation input stops draining at the worst possible moment for a credibility-minded new chair.

## 🎯 Today's One Idea: No new position

Twelfth straight session with no new name, and the discipline is the point. The day's loudest event — the strait re-closing — is precisely the kind of headline that begs you to chase oil, and the price just told you not to: a 20%-of-supply chokepoint shut overnight and crude managed a four-dollar rebound. There is no edge in buying a war premium the market itself refuses to pay. The indium story has real legs but no clean, liquid, one-layer-down name with the kind of margin of safety this blog requires — "almost nobody refines it outside China" is a thesis, not yet an entry. And the standing power idea (GE Vernova) is already on the board; I won't double-count it into a hawkish-Fed multiple compression just because the build is intact. **What would change this:** a sustained, inventory-draining Hormuz closure that re-arms the premium the *physical* way (then safe-transit capacity becomes ownable again, not the barrel), or an indium move from "customs scrutiny" to formal control with an identifiable Western processor positioned to fill the gap. Neither is today.

## 🧠 One Thought

A week ago this blog spent paragraphs insisting you wait for the measured barrel and not the quotable verb, and the discipline was vindicated through three head-fakes. Today the lesson inverts and gets sharper: the chokepoint actually re-closed — the bad event the war-premium crowd spent four months pricing — and oil went up four dollars. The market has quietly stopped believing that closing Hormuz is scarce. That is what a regime change looks like from the inside: not a dramatic break, but the dog that doesn't bark. The same week, the input that genuinely *did* get scarcer was a zinc byproduct most people have never heard of, moving through Chinese customs forms instead of a strait. The bottleneck is always real — but it migrates, and the headline is almost always pointing at where it *used* to be. Hormuz spent four months teaching the world to fear the lane; the world finally learned the lesson on exactly the day the lane stopped mattering and a hyperscaler's optical-chip supply chain started.
