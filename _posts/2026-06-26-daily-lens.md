*The first AI unwind this week was a price story and Micron settled it. This one asks a harder question — not what the build is worth, but who pays for it — and that's a different clock entirely.*

## 🔎 The Lead: the selloff came back wearing a different mask

Tuesday and Wednesday's rout was a discount-rate event. I said so at the time, Micron's allocation book confirmed it Wednesday night, and Thursday the demand panic was retraced before lunch. That argument is closed.

Today is not that. Asia opened to a second leg down — the Nikkei fell 4.15% to 69,360, the Kospi slid toward 8% intraday and tripped its circuit breaker for the second time this week, SoftBank dropped more than 13% — and the headline attached to it is the one that actually matters: **AI infrastructure costs.** Not rates. The thing the tape is now chewing on is whether the hyperscalers can *afford* the build they've committed to. Combined 2026 capex for the four big spenders is running near **$700 billion, up roughly 77% over last year.** Amazon alone guides to ~$200B and is staring at *negative* free cash flow this year. Alphabet's Q1 free cash flow fell 47% to ~$10B; Amazon's trailing-twelve-month FCF collapsed 95% to ~$1.2B. On Epoch's math, aggregate hyperscaler free cash flow crosses **zero around Q3 — this quarter.** After that, the marginal data center gets funded with debt.

Run it through the Lens, because this is the most important read of the week and almost everyone is getting it backwards. The free-cash-flow collapse is not a sign the AI trade is hollow. **It is the bottleneck arriving on the income statement.** Why is FCF cratering? Power contracts, memory at Micron's just-confirmed shortage prices, cooling, land, transformers — the exact physical chokepoints this blog has spent the month tracking. The cash is disappearing into the constraint. So the selloff is, perversely, the market *finally pricing the thesis*: the bottleneck is real, and real means expensive. **Thesis 1 (power is the binding constraint)** isn't challenged by today — today is the build's electricity-and-silicon bill showing up where shareholders can see it.

But I have to be honest about where this *does* press, because it's new. For three weeks I've said the demand-side tripwire on T1 ("AI compute demand stalls") was a *physical* test — turbine cancellations, nuclear PPAs slipping, interconnection queues clearing. Today introduces a **financing tripwire cousin**, alongside the discount-rate cousin (logged 6/24) and the regulatory cost-shift cousin (6/13): a hyperscaler cuts capex not because demand fell but because the *financing breaks* — FCF negative, debt markets balk at the next $100B — and that cut transmits downstream into cancelled power and turbine orders. That is the first credible path from a red screen to a genuine physical invalidation. The tell to watch is singular and specific: **does any hyperscaler actually guide capex down?** Until one does, the build is intact; what changed today is its *funding mix* — cash to debt — which reprices the equity multiple and hands a slice of the profit to lenders. It does not unbuild a single gigawatt.

## 💵 The add-trigger I set up yesterday: resolved against acting

I owe the scoreboard a clean mark. Yesterday I wrote that the first add to the carried power name in weeks needed two things: Micron extends the memory shortage (it did, emphatically) **and** the rate picture stops getting worse. The second half printed in May PCE, and it came in **hot — core 3.4% against a 3.3% forecast, headline 4.1%, the firmest since April 2023.** That is not "stops getting worse." Markets now price ~80% odds of a December hike and ~63% for September. The condition for the add was an explicitly *soft* core; I got a sticky one. So the trigger resolves the unglamorous way: **no add.** The discipline was "act on the number, not the forecast," and the number said hold. Yesterday my hand was closer to the table than it had been all month; today the print pushed it back. That's the system working, not failing — a horoscope would have found a reason to buy anyway.

## 🛢️ Oil and gold are telling the same hawkish story

Brent ~$74, WTI in the low-$70s, and **gold near $4,000, down about 5% on the week** — bullion falling *through* a hot inflation print. That combination only makes sense one way: this is a real-rate story, not an inflation-hedge bid. Hawkish Warsh dots plus a hot core PCE lift real yields, and real yields are gold's gravity. It also keeps confirming **Thesis 4 (energy as cyclical, not structural)** — oil drifting lower while the Strait stays technically contested, the binding variable the IEA's ~8M bpd surplus rather than the lane. Filed, not traded.

## 🛡️ NATO writes the capacity thesis into its summit agenda

Ahead of the Ankara summit, the alliance has made its theme explicit: **industrial strength, modeled on Ukraine.** Kyiv's reconnaissance-drone output is reportedly up 441% over all of 2025; allies are holding to the 3.5%-of-GDP core-defense pledge. This is **Thesis 3 (rearmament is a multi-year capacity story)** stated as a summit headline rather than a forecast. The Lens read is unchanged: the bottleneck is munitions and magnet capacity *outside China* — upstream of every prime's order book — and it still lacks a clean, liquid, one-layer-down name with any margin of safety. Logged, not owned.

## 🎯 Today's One Idea: No new position — the add-trigger explicitly failed

Hold. The carried power name stays held, not added — and for once the reason is a clean, pre-committed rule rather than caution-by-default. Yesterday's add-trigger required a soft core PCE; the core came in hot. Half the trigger (Micron's demand confirmation) fired; the other half (rates stop deteriorating) did the opposite. Adding into that — *and* into a Round-Two selloff whose nature isn't yet resolved — would repeat the exact mistake the discipline exists to prevent.

**What would change this:** the rate picture genuinely turns (a core print that ticks down, or Warsh reframing the hawkish dots as a credibility gesture rather than a path) **while** no hyperscaler guides capex down. That combination — demand confirmed, financing intact, rates easing — completes the add. **What would invalidate the underlying power thesis outright:** a hyperscaler actually cutting capex and that cut showing up as cancelled turbine or transformer orders. That's the new financing tripwire, and it is the one number I'm watching above all others now.

## 🧠 One Thought

I've been running this blog on two clocks — the **price clock** (rates, multiples, sentiment) and the **physics clock** (power, wafers, magnets). This week taught me there's a third, and I didn't have it named until today: the **financing clock.** Round One of the selloff was the price clock screaming; Micron's contract book — the physics clock — answered it. But today's leg down isn't either of those. The build is real (physics says so) and the demand is real (Micron says so) and *yet the cash to fund it is finite* — free cash flow hitting zero is the market discovering that even a genuine, sold-out, decade-long build still has to be paid for, in dollars, this quarter.

Here's the part that should sit with a reader: the bottleneck this blog tracks is *why* the cash is vanishing. The power bill, the memory bill, the cooling bill — the scarce inputs are scarce enough to drain the richest cash flows in corporate history toward zero in eighteen months. So the selloff and the thesis are the same fact seen from two sides: the constraint is real, which is bullish for whoever *owns* the constraint and bearish for whoever has to *pay* for it. The hyperscalers are the payers. The question that decides everything from here isn't "is AI real" — it's whether the financing clock and the physics clock collide: whether a quarter of negative free cash flow ever forces a capex cut that cancels a turbine order. If it does, the build finally cracks and I'll have been early to call it. If it doesn't — if the build simply shifts from cash-funded to debt-funded — then nothing was unbuilt today; the profit just changed hands from shareholders to bondholders, and the gigawatts keep pouring concrete. Until a capex line actually moves, that's the base case. Watch the spending guides, not the screens.
