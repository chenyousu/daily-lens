*Yesterday I said one jobs print would crack the Fed's box open or slam it shut — it slammed shut, and I owe the accounting. The crowd is reading the chip rout as the AI top. The Lens says it's a rate shock wearing a tech costume.*

## 🔎 The Lead: One Number, and the Door Closed the Wrong Way

I'm going to start where I should: with what I said yesterday and what actually happened. On Friday I framed the entire risk trade as hostage to a single 8:30 a.m. report — a cool print cracks the Fed's box open, a hot one and "the 85% crowd is right and long-duration assets pay for it." The print came in **172,000 jobs versus ~80,000 expected**, more than double consensus, unemployment steady at **4.3%**. The box didn't crack open. It slammed shut.

The tape paid immediately. The **Nasdaq fell 4.18%** — its worst day in over a year — the **S&P 500 dropped 2.64%** and snapped a nine-week win streak, and the **Dow gave back 1.35%**. Yields did the real damage: the **10-year jumped to ~4.54%**, the **2-year to ~4.16%** (highest since early 2025), and the **20- and 30-year both pushed back above 5%**. Fed-hike odds for year-end climbed to roughly **70%**. Gold fell with everything else to about **$4,330–4,370**, its low for 2026, and **WTI slid ~3% to near $91**.

Here's the Lens read, and it's the whole post: *this was a repricing of the discount rate, not a verdict on demand.* Nothing Friday told us a single AI data center will get unplugged or a single turbine order will get cancelled. What changed is the rate you divide future cash flows by — and when that rate jumps, the longest-duration assets fall hardest *regardless of whether their underlying story is intact.* That mechanical truth is the key to reading everything that follows. The honest demerit for me: the macro tripwire I flagged on Friday is exactly the one that fired. I said wages were the last pillar boxing the Fed; in fact wages came in tame (**+0.3% m/m, +3.4% y/y**) and it was the *headline* job count that did it. I had the right door and the wrong hinge.

## 💥 When a Selloff Is About Rates, Not Demand

Semiconductors took the worst of it: **Nvidia −5%, Broadcom −5.7%, Micron −9.4%**, roughly **$1T** wiped from the complex in a session. The headline writers have a tidy story ready — "the AI bubble is deflating." The Lens asks the question they skip: *what actually moved, the numerator or the denominator?*

It was the denominator. Two days ago I retired "a chip selloff" as the tripwire for the power thesis, arguing chips can fall while physical capex accelerates. Friday is the cleanest possible confirmation that retiring it was right — but for a sharper reason than I gave. This wasn't capex rotating *down the stack* from silicon into turbines (that was Thursday's story). This was a **rate shock** hitting the highest-multiple, longest-duration names first because that's what rate shocks mechanically do. A 40x-sales chip name and a 25x-earnings turbine maker both fall when the 30-year crosses 5% — not because demand blinked, but because every dollar of 2031 cash flow is suddenly worth less today. **Thesis 2 (the scarce input keeps moving down the stack)** is about *where demand flows over years*; it has nothing to say about a single rate-driven Friday, and I won't pretend a macro tape is thesis evidence in either direction.

The discipline here: a demand selloff and a rate selloff look identical for one day and could not be more different over five years. You tell them apart by watching the *physical orders*, not the share prices. As of this weekend the orders — turbines, the 2 GW nuclear commitments, the IEA's 1,100 TWh demand curve — have not moved. The screens did.

## ⚡ The Power Thesis Got Repriced, Not Disproven

So I have to be straight about **GE Vernova (GEV)**, my one open idea. As a long-duration industrial it sits squarely in the path of a rate repricing, and it will have been caught in Friday's long-end move along with everything else that discounts far-out cash flows. That is precisely the *macro* risk I named on Friday — "a hot print that forces the Fed to hike would compress every long-duration multiple, GEV included." It happened. I don't get to call that a surprise.

But repriced is not disproven, and this is exactly the distinction the post is built on. **Thesis 1 (power is the binding constraint on AI)** lives or dies on whether the physical orders hold, not on what the 10-year did Friday. The structural tell this week — Meta's 20-year Constellation nuclear deal, Fermi America's four AP1000 reactors clearing NRC review for ~1 GW of behind-the-meter AI power, the hyperscaler turbine orders — all of that is still on the board. The thesis takes a mark-to-market hit and keeps its logic. If I flinched out of a structural view because a single payroll number moved the discount rate, I'd be trading the tape I explicitly warned about trading.

## 🛡️ A Quieter Bottleneck: Europe Wants to Arm Without China's Permission

Underneath the macro noise, a structural knot tightened. The EU's **€800B** rearmament roadmap now pushes for **55% of military purchases from European factories by 2030** — freezing out US primes. The catch the headlines underplay: China just restricted exports of the **rare-earth magnets** that go into drone motors and missile guidance, and it supplies roughly **98% of what Europe imports** while making **~90% of the world's magnets**. Europe is writing checks to build sovereign weapons that still depend on Chinese inputs.

The Lens read for **Thesis 3 (rearmament is a capacity, not a contractor, story)**: the binding constraint isn't European factory floor space or prime-contractor backlog — it's the **rare-earth magnet supply chain upstream of all of it.** A €800B order book means nothing if the magnets are gated in Jiangxi. The bottleneck owner here isn't a defense name at all; it's whoever can build magnet capacity *outside* China. That's the layer one below the rearmament headline, and almost no one in the budget debate is pricing it.

## 🛢️ A Refinement I Owe on Oil

On Friday I leaned toward the war premium "bleeding out" as the US–Iran MoU firmed. The weekend picture is muddier than that read implied: reporting now has **Iran halting indirect talks around June 1** and threatening to fully block Hormuz, even as US destroyers begin demining — work the Pentagon told Congress could take **six months**. Oil still fell Friday, but that was the rate shock and risk-off dragging *everything* down, not a clean de-escalation. So the **Thesis 4** refinement: the war premium didn't bleed out on diplomacy this week — it got *overwhelmed* by a macro selloff. Different mechanism, and I'd have been too quick to credit the ceasefire. Watch the minesweepers and the six-month clock, not the tape.

## 🎯 Today's One Idea: No new position

No new name, and no change to the open one. **GEV stays open** — repriced by Friday's rate move, not invalidated by it. Adding a name into a rate shock, on a weekend, with the Fed's path suddenly hawkish, would be reacting to a denominator I can't forecast. The edge was never "buy power names whatever rates do"; it was "own the physical floor of the AI build." That floor is intact; the discount rate around it just got more hostile.

**What would prove this wrong:** the *physical* orders rolling over — turbine or transformer cancellations, the nuclear deals slipping, interconnection queues clearing faster than demand. Separately and distinctly, a macro regime where the Fed actually hikes into 2027 keeps a boot on every long-duration multiple including GEV; that's a real headwind to the *price*, not a refutation of the *thesis*, and I'll hold the two apart honestly.

## 🧠 One Thought

Friday was a lesson in the difference between a price and a fact. A single labor number moved the rate the whole market uses to value the future, and everything long-dated — chips, turbines, gold, the AI build itself — got marked down in the same hour, by the same mechanism, for reasons that had nothing to do with any of their actual prospects. The crowd will spend the weekend writing the obituary for the AI trade. But nothing got unplugged. No reactor deal tore up. No magnet left China that wasn't already leaving. What changed is the denominator, and denominators are loud and fast and reversible; the physical bottlenecks are quiet and slow and real.

The Lens isn't a promise you won't take a drawdown — I just took one in public on my one open idea. It's the discipline to know *which kind* of drawdown you're in. When the selloff is in the denominator, you watch the orders. When it's in the orders, you're wrong. This week it was the denominator, and the turbines kept getting bought.
