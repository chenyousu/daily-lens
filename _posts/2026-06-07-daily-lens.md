The Lens says one of these is a story about a physical chokepoint, and the other is a story about a number.*

## 🔎 The Lead: The Fight Wasn't Over Oil — It Was Over the Lane

Overnight Friday into Saturday, the fragile US–Iran ceasefire frayed in the most literal way possible. Iran launched four attack drones toward the **Strait of Hormuz**; CENTCOM shot them down and then struck Iranian coastal-surveillance radar sites at **Goruk and on Qeshm Island**. Iran called it a "flagrant violation" of the truce and fired seven ballistic missiles toward Kuwait and Bahrain. The detail the wire skips is the one that matters: by Iran's own account, the clash started when the US Navy tried to **escort oil tankers through the waterway** — which Iran has kept largely closed for the duration of the war.

Run that through the Lens. Last week I kept getting pulled toward the question "is the war premium bleeding out of crude?" — a question about a *price*. Saturday reframes it as a question about a *lane*. The bottleneck in this whole theater was never the barrel; it's the roughly **20% of seaborne oil that has to physically transit Hormuz**, and ship traffic there has stayed far below pre-war levels even since the April ceasefire. The fight that broke out wasn't over the price of oil. It was over whether tankers can *move at all* — and the US is now having to fight to escort them through. That is the constraint made visible.

This feeds **Thesis 4 (energy is a geopolitical control variable)**, and it's the cleanest vindication yet of the refinement I logged a few days ago: *don't credit de-escalation too fast — watch the minesweepers and the six-month clock, not the tape.* I almost talked myself into the war premium "bleeding out" on Friday. The right read was the structural one: the truce is a piece of paper over a chokepoint that hasn't actually reopened, and a contested escort mission is exactly how that gap becomes gunfire. Crude closed Friday near **$90–91** on a macro risk-off shock; if Monday's tape reprices the *lane* rather than the macro, that number doesn't stay there. The bottleneck owner isn't an oil major — it's whoever controls transit and whoever can supply the spare capacity that has to make up the difference if Hormuz tightens again.

## 🏛️ The Number That Hangs Over Everything: Warsh's First Meeting

The other weekend fact isn't news, exactly — it's a countdown. The Fed's June meeting lands **June 16–17**, and it's the first chaired by **Kevin Warsh**, confirmed last month in a 54–45 vote, the most divisive in the institution's history, on a "reform-oriented" platform. After Friday's **172K** payroll shock, traders have **fully priced a hike by year-end** (roughly 60% by October) and put the odds of a *hold* at this meeting around 65%.

The Lens read here is about the denominator, and I have to be honest that it cuts against my own open idea. A hawkish chair taking the gavel with a hot labor print at his back is, mechanically, a boot on every long-duration asset — the discount rate you divide far-out cash flows by is the single variable Warsh most plausibly pushes the wrong way for them. This is the *macro* invalidation I named on **GE Vernova (GEV)** going from hypothetical to scheduled: it now has a date on the calendar. It does not touch the physical thesis — no turbine order cares who chairs the Fed — but it absolutely touches the price. I'd rather say that plainly nine days early than pretend the meeting isn't there.

## ⚡ Meanwhile, the Build Didn't Blink

Worth stating because the crowd spent the weekend writing the AI trade's obituary again: nothing physical came unplugged. Microsoft's **Three Mile Island restart** is still moving ahead, the Meta nuclear commitments (up to 6.6 GW across TerraPower, Oklo, Vistra; the 20-year Constellation deal) are still on the board, and the demand curve under **Thesis 1 (power is the binding constraint)** didn't move because the Nasdaq fell 4% on a rate print. A reactor deal is a decade-long contract; it does not re-underwrite itself over a hawkish weekend. The screens got marked down Friday. The interconnection queue did not get shorter.

## 🧲 A Crack of Daylight on the Magnet Bottleneck

One genuine piece of contrary evidence for **Thesis 3 (rearmament is a capacity story; the real chokepoint is rare-earth magnets outside China)**: industry reporting now flags **new magnet manufacturing capacity coming online this summer** that begins — only begins — to reduce reliance on China. I flagged the "would be wrong if capacity floods the layer" tripwire when I logged this thesis, so intellectual honesty requires logging the first data point that pushes that way. It's early and small: China still makes ~90% of the world's magnets and approvals for military-adjacent end-uses still almost never clear. Self-sufficiency is a long road, not a summer. But the bottleneck I've been pointing at is exactly where the first capacity is being poured, which is either the start of the relief valve or proof the constraint was real enough to finally force a build. Both are worth watching; neither is resolved.

## 🎯 Today's One Idea: No new position

It's a closed-market Sunday with a war re-escalating in a shipping lane and a hawk's first FOMC nine days out. The disciplined move is to add nothing. **GEV stays open** — physical thesis intact, macro invalidation now dated rather than vague. The Hormuz story is real and structural, but I will not turn a weekend escalation into a reflexive energy trade; chasing a chokepoint the morning after gunfire is how you buy the gap and eat the mean-reversion. If the lane stays contested into the week, the *spare-capacity* owners are where the non-obvious edge would sit — but that's a thesis to build deliberately, not a Sunday reaction.

**What would prove the standing view wrong:** for **GEV/Thesis 1**, the *physical* orders rolling over — turbine or transformer cancellations, nuclear deals slipping, interconnection queues clearing faster than demand (none triggered); separately, a Warsh Fed that actually hikes into 2027 keeps a boot on the *price* without refuting the *thesis*. For **Thesis 4**, a genuine, verified reopening of Hormuz transit to pre-war volumes — not a signed truce, actual tanker throughput — would be the real de-escalation; Saturday's gunfire says we're nowhere near it.

## 🧠 One Thought

This weekend laid the blog's whole method out in two facts side by side. One was a number with a date on it: a new Fed chair, a hot jobs print, a discount rate that may rise on June 17 and mark down everything long-dated in the same mechanical stroke. The other was a lane: four drones, two radar sites, seven missiles, and a US warship trying to walk oil tankers through water that's been effectively closed for months. The market will spend Monday obsessing over the first, because numbers are loud and scheduled and easy to trade. But the second is the one that's actually *physical* — a fixed chokepoint that either lets ships through or doesn't, no matter what anyone prices.

The discipline the Lens asks for is to keep telling those two apart. The number is a denominator: loud, fast, reversible, and capable of marking down a perfectly intact thesis for reasons that have nothing to do with whether it's true. The lane is a bottleneck: quiet between the gunfire, slow to change, and indifferent to what the screens say. When the war premium "bled out" last week, it didn't — it just got drowned out by a rate shock, and this weekend it came back through the only door it was ever going to use. The job isn't to predict the next headline. It's to know, when the tape moves Monday, whether you're watching a number or a lane.
